The Complete Guide to Music PR for Independent Artists

Pillar guide · 12 min read

Music PR (public relations) is the work of getting journalists, bloggers, playlist curators and radio hosts to talk about your music. For independent artists it can feel mysterious and expensive, but the core of it is simple: give the right people a good reason to cover you, and make it easy for them to do so. This guide walks through the whole process, from preparing your materials to measuring what worked.

What music PR actually is

PR is earned attention. Unlike advertising, where you pay for space, PR relies on someone else deciding your music or story is worth sharing. That is why coverage carries more trust with readers, and also why it cannot be guaranteed. Your job is to improve the odds.

Step 1: Get your foundation ready

Before you contact anyone, prepare the material editors will ask for. At minimum you need a finished, publicly streamable track, a short and long bio, two or three high-quality press photos with credits, a one-paragraph description of the release, and links to your social profiles. Put these together in an EPK so everything lives in one place. If you only do one thing from this guide, do this. A pitch with missing links or low-quality images often gets skipped even when the music is strong.

Step 2: Find your story

Editors cover stories, not just songs. Ask yourself what is interesting about this release. Maybe it was recorded in an unusual place, written after a major life change, or made with a notable collaborator. Maybe you are part of a local scene that is getting attention. A clear angle gives a writer something to build a paragraph around, and gives you a hook for every pitch.

Step 3: Build a targeted outlet list

A list of twenty relevant outlets beats a blast to two hundred random ones. Look at who covers artists like you, check that they have posted recently, and read their submission guidelines. Note the editor's name where possible. Include a mix of small blogs, genre-specific sites, magazines, playlist curators and community radio shows. Smaller outlets are more likely to respond to unknown artists and their coverage still builds your credibility.

Step 4: Pitch with care

A good pitch is short. Introduce yourself in a sentence, say what the release is and why it fits that outlet, and include your links. Keep it under 150 words and avoid attachments. Personalise the opening line so it is clear you know the outlet. Send your pitch two to four weeks before release when the outlet accepts unreleased music, or on release day when it does not.

Step 5: Use free submission networks

Submitting individually takes time. Free networks like BRiNSPOT let you submit your bio, release and photos once, and our editors share approved submissions with magazines, blogs and digital outlets. It does not replace your own outreach, but it widens your reach without cost.

Step 6: Follow up and say thank you

Wait about a week, then send one brief follow-up that adds something new, such as a video or a live date. When an outlet features you, share the article, tag them and thank the writer. People remember artists who promote the outlets that support them, and that goodwill makes your next release easier to place.

Step 7: Reuse and measure

Add every piece of coverage to your EPK and website, since press builds on itself. Track where your listeners and followers come from using the analytics in Spotify for Artists, Bandcamp and your social accounts. Over time you will see which types of outlet send real listeners, and you can focus your effort there.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Pitching before your music and photos are ready
  • Sending the same generic email to everyone
  • Paying for guaranteed coverage, streams or "viral" results
  • Giving up after one release instead of building relationships

PR is a long game. Artists who release consistently, pitch thoughtfully and treat editors with respect tend to see their coverage grow with every release.
